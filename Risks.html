1. Safety & Ethical Risks:

AVs introduce new safety concerns, including less cautious behavior from occupants and road users, system errors, and the lack of clear regulations for “crash algorithms” that determine life-or-death decisions in unavoidable accidents caused by human error, machine errors, and their unpredictable nature remain challenges.

Autonomous Vehicles face several major safety risks because they operate in a complex, unpredictable environments and rely heavily on sensors, predictive models and decision-making algorithms. One important safety risk comes from perception uncertainty. Sensors like cameras, LiDAR and radar can be affected by weather, lighting, or hardware limitations. It will become the “dangerous feature” on the road which can harm both users and pedestrians when the system fails to accurately detect vehicles, lane markings, obstacles or pedestrians.

Another concern is about the safety of this new transportation in decision-making. Decision-making becomes risky when system cannot confidently evaluate the consequences of different actions, when objectives conflict or when the environment changes too quickly for the algorithms to response. And especially when crash happens, the systems will prioritize passenger safety, or pedestrian safety.

Compared to traditional driving way, human is more flexible in some external effects, such as faulty traffic lights, roads fixings, uncertainty road lines and many more human situations that machines may not interpret correctly.

For example, the uncertainty of autonomous systems is heavily illustrated by the 2016 Tesla Autopilot fatality. A prominent case study revealed that the crash was primarily a "designer error" rather than user negligence; the vehicle’s sensor suite failed to perceive a white tractor-trailer against a bright sky, highlighting a severe limitation in the machine's situational awareness (Banks, Plant, & Stanton, 2018).

2. Privacy & Cybersecurity Risks:

The remarkable concern is about data collection. The rise of Avs brings privacy risks related to data storage and transmission, and unauthorized third-party access (Alam, 2024). Due to the massive volumes of highly sensitive data these vehicles used, including personal information, real-time location tracking, Face ID, cabin audio, bank account, and many more data be used in this system. A major privacy risk stems from the fact that the vehicle captures data not just from the owner, but from external data subjects, including passengers, children, pedestrians, and cyclists. This continuous data collection creates a risk of unauthorized surveillance, corporate data monetization, and permanent digital profiling if the data is intercepted or poorly governed.

One major cyberattack risk is remote hacking of vehicles controls, where attacks exploit vulnerabilities in the vehicle’s internal systems to manipulate critical functions such as steering, braking, or acceleration. Another risk comes from connectivity attacks. Autonomous vehicles constantly communicate via Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and cellular networks. Each of these channels can be exploited by attackers to intercept data, inject malicious messages, or spoof signals such as GPS locations or traffic alerts. Because these systems are always online, the attack surface becomes much larger. A further risk involves sensor manipulation, where attackers interfere with the vehicle’s perception of the environment. Attackers can use techniques such as adversarial stickers on road signs, lasers to blind LiDAR, or spoofed radar signals, which cause unsafe decisions, including sudden stops or failure to recognize obstacles.

Modern vehicle manufacturers are facing criticism and allegations that they are focusing more on selling data obtained from vehicles rather than on the actual vehicle sales, in line with the widespread data processing and transfer activities. In this regard, various class-action lawsuits have been filed in the US, and investigations have been launched against vehicle manufacturers (Legalink,2025). Compared to the human-driven vehicles, autonomous ones introduces an entirely new digital vulnerability which raises much more privacy and cyberattack concerns.

3. Liability & Insurance Risks:

Autonomous vehicles raise significant liability and insurance risks because it becomes difficult to determine who is responsible when an accident occurs. One major liability risk arises from the difficulty of assigning fault. If an autonomous vehicle causes a collision, it is unclear whether the responsibility lies with the vehicle owner, the manufacturer, the software developer, the sensor supplier, or even the third-party provider of real-time mapping and connectivity services.Compared to traditional driving, the driver is almost held liable, but in autonomous vehicles there is no driver. Because there is no human driver to blame, this shift creates profound legal uncertainty regrading who should be held responsible for damages and injuries. This lack of clarify forces insurance companies to completely restructure their business models by selling car insurance directly to the technology and car corporations instead of individuals drivers.

4. Employment Risks:

Because of the potential to replace many jobs that currently rely on human driving, autonomous vehicles are already causing job-loss fears. Due to the more capable of these vehicles, jobs such as taxi drivers, truck drivers, delivery drivers or even bus drivers may decline sharply. A major US study found that 85% of Americans believe autonomous vehicles will cause job losses, especially for taxi, ride share, and delivery drivers. The study also warns that robotaxis could worsen income inequality and trigger public backlash because they “literally replace people.” (Ng,2026).

In China, drivers are publicly expressing fear that robotaxis are “snatching rice bowls,” meaning taking away their livelihoods. Taxi drivers in Wuhan even petitioned authorities to limit robotaxi operations because they were losing customers. (Chiang, 2024)
