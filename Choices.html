1. Level of Autonomy:

Semi-autonomous vehicles allow users to take control during emergencies or difficult situations. One benefit of this option is that it will increases safety because humans can intervene if the system makes a mistake. Especially, when the system is broken down or cannot make decision immediately. Another advantage is that this choice can raise human trust on the technology more since they still have some control in emergencies. However, a disadvantage is that drivers may become distracted or rely too much on the system, which can lead to the delay on reaction times in emergencies.

Full driving autonomation (Level 5) is the ultimate, fully autonomous vehicle. It can navigate all roads and weather conditions just as well as human driver, anywhere in the world, without needing a steering wheel or pedals, and the vehicle’s computer handles all emergency maneuvers and system failures on its own. But it can become a potential risk when human cannot take control during an unforeseen system failure or extreme emergency. Another ethical risks of fully autonomous vehicles include pre-programmed life-or-death decision making, algorithmic bias that threatens minority pedestrian safety.

2. Ethical Decision Models:

A minimize harm model is designed to reduce the overall physical damage and severity of road traffic injuries during unavoidable accident. The primary advantage of this model is that it focuses on protecting the greatest number of human lives by steering into the collision path calculated to yield the fewest total fatalities, matching the utilitarian principle of maximizing overall social good (Hevelke & Nida-Rümelin, 2015). However, a severe disadvantage is that passengers may feel unsafe or refuse to purchase the vehicle if they know the software is programmed to actively sacrifice them to save a larger group of external individuals (Bonnefon et al., 2016).

A passenger safety model and pedestrian safety model are both attempts to protect a specifically programmed group, such as passengers or pedestrians, during unavoidable accidents, which means the pedestrian-priority system focuses on protecting the people outside, while the passenger-priority system focuses on safety of people inside the vehicle. This approach helps the vehicles to follow clear and predictable ethical rules. But it may increase the risk for the other group, which can raise ethical concerns and public disagreement.

3. Data Collection Framework:

A minimal data collection framework ensures that the vehicle platform only stores and processes the essential information strictly required for immediate mechanical operation. A benefit of this kind of data collection is the significant improvement in passenger privacy and minimizing the societal footprint of data leaks or unauthorized corporate profiling. Additionally, automotive companies can reduce their expenditures on massive data storage infrastructure and high-bandwidth network transmission. Otherwise, this framework lead to limited data which may reduce the manufacturer’s ability to improve and train machine learning systems, or accurately reconstruct events during multiparty accident investigations.

Extentsive data collection framework is an alternative data collection choice which systematically harvests and archives a deep digital footprint, including location history, detailed passenger metrics, cabin video feeds, and localized travel patterns. Continuous data harvesting can dramatically improve predictive navigation models, tailors the customer ride experience, and speeds system-wide algorithms learning. Furthermore, these comprehensive datasets provide invaluable evidence to assist law enforcement agencies and streamline complex insurance investigations.

4. Technology Tools:

Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) technology powers the “eyes” of autonomous vehicles. LiDAR principle is pretty easy to understand: the system measures how long it takes for laser pulses to bounce off objects and return to the sensor. This simple concept creates detailed 3D maps with exceptional precision – about 0.1 degrees of spatial resolution (YellowScan, 2025). Companies such as Waymo use LiDAR because of its reliability. However, LiDAR systems are expensive and may perform poorly in heavy rain or fog, making vehicles more costly and less accessible to society.

Camera-only systems use visual data from multiple cameras combined with AI image recognition software. One advantage of this approach is lower production cost and easier large-scale deployment because cameras are cheaper and more compact than LiDAR systems. Tesla supports this camera-only approach, using neural networks and camera inputs to estimate depth and movement. However, camera systems depend heavily on lighting and weather conditions, which may reduce safety in difficult environments.

Radar systems use radio waves to detect nearby objects and work effectively in poor weather conditions. Radar is cheaper than LiDAR and improves safety through additional sensing support. However, radar provides less detailed environmental information compared to LiDAR.

The choice between these technologies shows that autonomous vehicle companies must balance safety, affordability, and reliability when implementing AI transportation systems in society.

5. Monetization and Market Control:

A low-cost monetization approach focuses on making autonomous vehicles affordable and accessible to a larger number of people. By reducing the manufacturing cost can make the technology more widespread and popular in society. It may also encourage faster adoption and improve public transportation access. However, the other face of this choice reduces quality of the systems and affect safety features of this technology.

A premium monetization approach focuses on selling autonomous vehicles as expensive, high-end products targeted mainly at wealthy customers. On of the advantage is that companies can earn higher profits and invest more in research, development, and advanced technology. However, it will have the limited access from average people, increase inequality and slow the widespread adoption.

Although premium monetization may accelerate technological innovation through higher research funding, it risks widening transportation inequality. Therefore, governments may need to regulate autonomous vehicle access to ensure the technology benefits society broadly rather than only wealthy consumers
